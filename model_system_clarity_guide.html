What your model actually does — and doesn't do

Your model learned one thing from the Kaggle dataset: the relationship between a budget amount, a department type, a month, and how much gets spent. That's it. It's not a crystal ball. It's a spending efficiency predictor.

What it CAN do ✓
Predict spending utilization ratio
Given IT + March + ₱40,000 budget → predicts they'll spend ₱43,200 (108% of budget). This ratio — how much of a budget gets consumed — is real and useful. Different departments and months produce different ratios because the model learned those patterns from history.
Detect seasonal overspend risk
The model knows that December tends to produce higher spending than April across most departments. It learned this from the monthly patterns in training data. This seasonal signal is genuinely useful for flagging which months Finance Managers should watch more closely.
Tell you if a budget cap is sufficient
If Main Admin sets IT's annual cap at ₱300,000 (₱25,000/month), the model can flag: "historically, IT-type departments tend to spend 108% of their monthly budget in December — this cap will likely be breached." That's a real warning before the fiscal year even starts.
Provide a risk label per department per month
Low / Medium / High risk based on whether predicted spending exceeds the budget. This is the most direct output and the most immediately actionable for Finance Managers reviewing approvals.
What it CANNOT do ✗
Predict future spending trends over time
It cannot say "spending will grow from ₱38k in January to ₱52k by June." That requires time series modeling (ARIMA, Prophet, LSTM) trained on YOUR system's own historical spending data — data that grows as NexaPlan is used. Your model doesn't have this.
Account for specific proposals already in the system
It has no knowledge of the ₱80,000 server purchase proposal sitting in Approvals right now. It only knows general department + month patterns from the Kaggle dataset.
Learn from your own tenants' spending behavior
As Finance Managers use NexaPlan and reconcile expenses, the model doesn't update. It stays frozen at what it learned from Kaggle. Over time your own DB will have better data than the model — and that's actually an opportunity for v2.
The right mental model: think of it like a doctor using population statistics to assess a patient's risk. "People with this profile tend to overspend in December" — it's a useful baseline signal, not a personalized prediction. Useful? Yes. Perfect? No.
The forecasting chart — what each line should actually come from

This is the most important thing to get right. The trajectory chart has four visual elements. Here is the honest answer for where each one should come from in your system:

⬤ Black line — Actual
Sum of reconciled Expense.Amount per month, grouped by SpentDate (the new date field you're adding). This is real money that was confirmed spent.
Real DB data
- - Dashed — Budget
Sum of DepartmentAllocations.TotalAllocatedCap ÷ 12 per month. The ceiling the organization set. Already in your DB.
Real DB data
⬤ Purple — Projected
Sum of approved BudgetProposal.RequestedAmount grouped by PlannedMonth. This is real committed spending your system already knows about — proposals that were approved and are scheduled for specific months.
Real DB data
▓ Purple band — Confidence
Upper bound = approved proposals + pending proposals (worst case all get approved). Lower bound = approved proposals only (best case nothing extra gets approved). This is a real data range — not ML uncertainty.
Real DB data
Notice: the ML model is not in any of these four lines. That's correct. Your model does not belong in the trajectory chart because the chart needs time-based trend data, and your model doesn't produce time-based trends.
So where does the model appear in the chart?

The model appears as a risk overlay on top of the chart — not as a line itself. Specifically:

How the model enhances the chart without being a chart line
Risk dot
For each future month on the chart, a colored dot appears above the projection line — green, amber, or red — based on the model's risk prediction for that month. Finance Manager sees at a glance which months are dangerous.
Warning flag
When hovering over a month tooltip, the model's prediction appears: "ML model predicts 108% utilization for IT in December — High risk." This gives context beyond just the committed proposal amounts.
AI Insights feed
The cards on the right side of the chart are entirely model-powered — they aggregate risk signals across departments and months and present them as actionable warnings. This is the model's primary visible output.
The chart becomes more powerful this way: the lines show real committed data (what you know will happen), and the model adds a risk signal layer on top (what historically tends to happen). Two sources of truth, each doing what they're best at.

This is pure math from your DB — no model needed. It tells you what happened. Red bar = overspent, green = underspent.
Where the model adds value to variance analysis
Use 1
Contextualizing the variance — was it predictable?
IT overspent by ₱3,200 in December. Was this a surprise or predictable? The model says: "IT in December historically has 108% utilization." So the variance was foreseeable — the problem was the budget cap was set too low, not that IT behaved unusually. This context changes what action the Finance Manager takes.
Use 2
Predicted vs actual variance — anomaly detection
Model predicted IT would spend 108% (₱43,200) but they actually spent ₱67,000. The gap between predicted and actual (₱23,800) is an anomaly — something happened beyond normal patterns. This flags IT for investigation. Without the model, you'd just see "IT overspent" — with it, you see "IT overspent way beyond even the expected overspend."

Adding SpentDate to reconciliation — why this matters

Right now your Expenses table tracks that money was spent, but not when it was spent. Adding a SpentDate field unlocks almost everything in the forecasting and variance system.

What changes with SpentDate
Without SpentDate (now)

Finance Manager reconciles a receipt.

No way to know if it was March or December.

The honest assessment of your model's role
Your model is not the engine of your forecasting system — your own DB data is the engine. The model is the risk intelligence layer on top of that engine. It adds a signal that your DB alone cannot provide: "based on historical patterns of how departments of this type behave in this month, here is the expected spending ratio and here is the risk level." That signal is real, it is useful, and it is something no amount of DB queries can replicate without historical training data. Your paper's argument should be exactly this: the system combines data-driven projection (from real proposals and reconciliation) with ML-based risk signals (from the trained Random Forest) to give Finance Managers both what they know will happen and what history suggests might happen.
